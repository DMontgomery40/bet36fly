Where does a neural network's architecture come from, if nobody sits down and designs it?

A neuron is a single cell with a long branching arm that reaches out toward other cells. Where it makes contact, a junction called a synapse carries a signal across. A connectome is a map of those cells and those contacts, traced out of real tissue. At bet365 we have spent the last few months building on one of them, in a project we call bet three sixty fly.

Connectomes come in different sizes: some cover a single circuit, some a region, some a whole brain. The one behind this project covers a male fruit fly's brain together with the ventral nerve cord that runs down into its body — roughly a hundred and sixty-seven thousand neurons, in a volume smaller than a sesame seed.

Recovering a map like that is slow physical work. The tissue is preserved and sliced far thinner than a wavelength of light, and every slice goes under an electron microscope. What comes back is a stack of tens of thousands of images in which a neuron appears only as a cross-section, a smudge that has to be matched to the smudge above it and the smudge below it, through every branch and every turn, past thousands of neighboring cells doing the same thing. Google Research built the machine-learning systems that draft those tracings, working with HHMI Janelia, the University of Cambridge, and the MRC Laboratory of Molecular Biology, whose scientists proofread and corrected the result. The imaging and tracing methods behind it were developed over decades. This particular resource went public on the eighth of June this year, and the paper followed on the third of September.

What an engineer downloads at the end of all that is a graph. Neurons are the nodes, connections are the edges, and each edge carries the number of synaptic contacts the two cells make with each other. The graph arrives with structure already in it: dedicated routes for vision, for smell, for driving legs and wings, for holding onto something that happened a moment ago. Nobody drew that layout on a whiteboard. It is there because it kept flies alive.

A graph does not do anything on its own. It tells you which cells can send signals to which, and the rest has to be supplied: how much voltage builds in a modeled neuron as inputs arrive, how much charge crosses a synapse, when the cell fires, and how quickly it settles afterward. Those equations come out of the experimental literature — fly neurons have been recorded from for decades, and published spiking models of fly circuits are already there to work from. So the wiring comes out of the animal, the timing comes out of a simulator, and running the thing means running both.

Which brings us to the awkward part. A fruit fly's nervous system has no input for baseball. It has inputs for light, for odor molecules, for the taste of something sweet and the taste of something bitter, for the position of its own legs. So the first engineering problem in bet three sixty fly is the adapter. We use the taste pathways. The pregame numbers for a team — recent form, the starting pitcher, the ordinary inputs any baseball model takes — become patterns of stimulation on the appetitive and aversive channels, an engineered version of putting something sweet or something sharp in front of the animal. Both teams are encoded by the same rules and presented separately, so one team tasting good never requires the other to taste bad, and nothing in the input tells the circuit in advance which one to favor.

Then you run it and watch. Activity spreads from the taste inputs through whatever the wiring allows, settles, and leaves a pattern across a set of neurons we chose in advance. That pattern is what we read out, and a small external model fit on past seasons turns the two patterns into a prediction. If you work with embeddings, the shape will be familiar: something goes in, a representation comes out of the middle, a head reads the representation. The middle here is a circuit with an anatomy, unfolding over simulated milliseconds.

Across the two thousand four hundred and twenty-three games of the 2023 Major League Baseball season, the frozen pipeline called about fifty-six percent of them correctly, level with a conventional model given the same pregame information. What the season really bought us is the environment. Every prediction now has a path through named cells, and those cells can be addressed one at a time. You can pick a pathway, quiet it, present the same matchup again, and see what the readout does — then do it a hundred more times with a different route switched off in each run.

The other thing a fly does, which the pipeline as described does not, is remember. A fly that finds food while a particular smell is in the air will treat that smell differently the next time it drifts past. The structure behind that is called the mushroom body, where sensory signals meet a teaching signal carried by dopamine. In a separate development model we built that in: pair a cue with the teaching signal, wait, then present the cue on its own, and the circuit answers it differently than it did before. The change stays specific to the cue that was trained, and it reverses when the teaching signal reverses. We have run that learning inside the sports pipeline as well; it has not produced a prediction improvement we would stand behind, and the mechanism is now sitting there, inside a circuit we can take apart.

We are not the only people who have taken this graph somewhere it was never meant to go. A developer wired it into Doom. Game frames go in as input, the code path runs through the retained connectome graph, and a fixed set of outputs is mapped onto moving and shooting. That code path is also how you tell a project like DOOMFLY apart from the flies crawling around people's feeds, because some of those are animations — a fly wanders a browser window with a dopamine meter ticking beside it, the behavior written in JavaScript, no neural model making any of the decisions. Looking biological on screen tells you nothing about what is driving it. With DOOMFLY you can open the repository and follow one frame all the way to a keypress, which means the same interventions are available there.

Doom also shows up what our own setup is missing, which is consequence. Our circuit is presented with something and responds, and nothing it does changes what arrives next. Two other projects fill in pieces of that loop. FlyVis takes the measured connectivity of the fly's visual system, optimizes a model of it on visual tasks, and compares its modeled cells against recordings from real ones. FlyBody is the other end: a fly's body in a physics engine, with reinforcement-learning controllers that make it walk and fly. Nobody has joined them into a single animal, and between them they mark out what a connectome-derived controller with eyes and legs would have to handle.

The reason all of this is arriving at once is that two very different timescales met. The imaging, the tracing, the proofreading — that is decades of method and years of work on this one specimen, finished in June. The experiments built on top of it are weeks old. Reading into an unfamiliar literature, writing an adapter, getting somebody else's research code to run used to be the slow parts, and coding agents built on large language models have made them cheap enough that a small team can cross from neuroscience to a running pipeline in days.

And the maps keep improving. A larval zebrafish is transparent, which means you can point a microscope at a living one and watch neurons light up across most of its brain while the animal behaves. Google Research's neural mapping program is reconstructing the wiring of a zebrafish brain in a project called Fish2, with the aim of pairing that wiring with activity recorded from the same specimen; the companion ZAPBench dataset already carries activity from more than seventy thousand neurons. For anyone writing the dynamics, that pairing changes the work. You can run your equations on the measured wiring, hold the result against what those same neurons actually did, and tighten the model where the two disagree.

Mammalian circuits are at an earlier stage, and that work is moving too. The MICrONS project reconstructed a block of mouse visual cortex holding something like half a billion synaptic contacts, with activity recordings from the same tissue, and larger mapping programs are running now. Every species brings its own biology, and what carries across is the practice: writing an interface into a sensory pathway, and designing an intervention that isolates one route through a circuit.

So here is what the next week looks like from inside this project. Somewhere in a hundred and sixty-seven thousand traced fly neurons there is a pathway that has never had a baseball game presented to it, and we can call it by name, silence it, and run Tuesday's matchup again. When the fish arrives, that same question will come with the animal's own recordings sitting beside it.
