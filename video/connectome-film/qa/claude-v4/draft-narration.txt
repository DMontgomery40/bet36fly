A fruit fly has roughly a hundred and sixty-seven thousand neurons in its brain and the nerve cord that connects the brain to its body. Those neurons form about fifty million connections. Each connection is a physical junction where one cell can pass a signal to another, and the whole arrangement fits in a volume smaller than a sesame seed. At bet three sixty-five, a team has taken the measured wiring of that nervous system and given it a job it was never built for: processing baseball matchups. The project is called bet three sixty fly.

Before that sentence makes any sense, you need one piece of biology. A connectome is a complete map of every neuron and every connection in one animal's nervous system. It is not a schematic or an approximation. It is the actual wiring recovered from one particular specimen: which cell connects to which, how many contact points they share, and where those contacts sit in the body. If a neural network you train is an architecture you chose, a connectome is an architecture you inherited from a real animal.

Recovering one is an enormous physical undertaking. Researchers slice preserved tissue into sections thinner than a wavelength of light and image each section with an electron microscope. The result is a stack of images, sometimes tens of thousands of slices, where every neuron appears as a cross-section. Tracing a single neuron means following its shape across hundreds or thousands of those slices while it branches, thins, and weaves past thousands of its neighbors. Google Research built machine-learning tools that draft those tracings automatically. Human scientists then check and correct every identification. For the male fruit fly resource used in bet three sixty fly, Google worked with HHMI Janelia, the University of Cambridge, and the MRC Laboratory of Molecular Biology. The dataset, covering the brain and the ventral nerve cord, was released in June 2026, with the accompanying paper published in September. Years of imaging and proofreading produced a resource that an engineer can now load as a graph: nodes are neurons, edges are connections, and edge weights are synapse counts.

If you work with large language models, you already have an intuition for what makes this strange. When you design a transformer, you choose the architecture—how many layers, how wide, what kind of attention—and then training sets the weights. Here, the graph of connections is the part that came out of the animal. It was measured, not chosen. It contains specialized pathways for vision, smell, movement, and memory, and those pathways exist because they kept a fly alive, not because a researcher decided on a block diagram.

What the connectome does not give you is dynamics. A wiring diagram tells you which neurons can talk to each other. It does not tell you how fast a signal crosses a connection, how a neuron accumulates input before it fires, or how experience changes a connection's strength over time. Those behaviors come from equations a modeler writes down—spiking rules, transmission delays, plasticity functions—and fits against whatever experimental measurements exist. In a simple spiking model, incoming signals raise or lower a neuron's voltage; enough excitation triggers a pulse to its neighbors; between inputs, the voltage decays. The connectome constrains which neurons can influence which. The equations you supply determine what happens when they do.

bet three sixty fly asks a narrow question: if you present pregame baseball information to this circuit and read out its response, does the response carry any information about who wins? The circuit has no concept of baseball, so the first engineering problem is an input interface. A fly does have circuitry for food-related signals—neurons that respond to something appetitive or something aversive. bet three sixty fly encodes pregame measurements as patterns of stimulation along those pathways: an engineered version of presenting something sweet or something bitter. Each team in a matchup gets its own stimulus, constructed from the same encoding rules, and neither stimulus presupposes which team will win. Two opportunities, translated into a language the circuit's own wiring can carry.

The response is read out from selected neurons after the circuit settles, and an external model is fit to predict the game outcome from that response. If you work with embeddings, the shape of this will be familiar: input, representation, readout. The difference is that the middle layer is not a learned embedding. It is a representation produced by an anatomically measured circuit unfolding over simulated time.

Across two thousand four hundred and twenty-three games from the 2023 Major League Baseball season, the frozen pipeline predicted correctly about fifty-six percent of the time. A conventional baseline using the same pregame information performed at the same level. The circuit did not beat it. The reason to keep going is that every prediction in bet three sixty fly can be traced backward through the circuit—which pathways carried the signal, which neurons responded, and how the response changed when you altered a connection. That transparency is what makes the next experiment possible, and a conventional model does not offer it.

That traceability is also what separates a neural-circuit experiment from a visual demo. Some of the fly animations circulating online are built in JavaScript: a fly wanders a screen with a dopamine meter beside it, and the behavior looks biological, but the movement is scripted and no neural model is making decisions. The visual appearance of neural control does not establish neural control. In a project like DOOMFLY, where a developer connected the released connectome graph to a first-person game, you can follow the code from game frame to neural input to circuit response to action. Change a pathway, replay the same input, and the behavior changes. That is the difference between illustration and experiment.

DOOMFLY also surfaces the next obvious limitation: bet three sixty fly presents stimuli and reads out a response, but the circuit's output never changes what it sees next. A game environment closes that loop. The circuit acts, the world responds, and the next input depends on what the circuit just did. Researchers have been building the components that loop requires. FlyVis constrains a model with measured fly visual connectivity, optimizes it on visual tasks, and compares its responses with recordings from real neurons. FlyBody provides a physically simulated fly body with reinforcement-learning controllers for walking and flight. Together, they point toward an environment where a connectome-derived controller faces real sensory consequences for its output.

One more component matters for any of these experiments: learning. In a separate model, a teaching signal was paired with a sensory cue and delivered to a region called the mushroom body, which is involved in associating cues with outcomes. After pairing, the cue alone produced a measurably different response, and the change was specific to that cue and reversible when the teaching signal changed. Connection strengths can saturate with repeated learning, and a recovery mechanism restores headroom for further change. These are early, controlled results from a development model. Whether learned associations improve prediction in bet three sixty fly is a separate research question that has not yet been tested.

All of this work has become much easier to attempt in the past year, because two things converged. Connectomics teams released richer maps, and coding agents built on large language models accelerated the work of reading papers, building interfaces, and testing software. A small team can now move across neuroscience, simulation, and software engineering at a pace that would have required a much larger group a few years ago. The recent burst of fly experiments—weeks of engineering built on years of reconstruction science—follows directly from that convergence.

The real payoff comes from richer animals. Young zebrafish are transparent enough that researchers can record neural activity across much of the living brain while the animal is behaving. Google's Fish2 program is working toward pairing a whole-brain wiring map with activity recordings from the same specimen. The associated ZAPBench resource already includes activity from more than seventy thousand neurons. That pairing changes the engineering fundamentally: the equations you had to invent for the fly become testable against what that animal's neurons actually did. Dynamics stop being a modeling choice and start being a calibration target. In mammalian tissue, the MICrONS project has reconstructed a volume of mouse visual cortex containing roughly half a billion synapses alongside linked activity recordings, and larger mapping programs are underway.

Each animal demands its own biology. But the engineering skills transfer: building input interfaces, reading out circuit responses, designing interventions, and connecting learning rules to outcomes. Working with the fly now is preparation for asking sharper questions when those larger, more thoroughly measured circuits arrive.

bet three sixty fly started with a measured nervous system and asked whether it could carry information about an entirely unfamiliar world. The answer so far is that the signal is comparable to a conventional model, every step is traceable through the circuit, and the system is ready for the next intervention. That is where the work is right now, and there is room for engineering teams to enter it.
