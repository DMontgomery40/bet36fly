What could you build with a neural network whose architecture came from an animal?

A neuron is a single cell with a long branching arm that reaches out toward other cells. Where it makes contact, a junction called a synapse carries a signal across. A connectome is a map of those cells and those contacts, traced out of real tissue. At bet three sixty-five, we have started building on one of them, in a project we call bet three sixty fly.

Connectomes come in different sizes: some cover a single circuit, some a region, some a whole brain. The one behind this project covers a male fruit fly's brain together with its ventral nerve cord, the bundle of nervous tissue running down into its body — roughly a hundred and sixty-seven thousand neurons, in a volume smaller than a sesame seed.

Recovering a map like that is slow physical work. The tissue is preserved and sliced far thinner than a wavelength of light, and every slice goes under an electron microscope. What comes back is a stack of tens of thousands of images in which a neuron appears only as a cross-section, a smudge that has to be matched to the smudge above it and the smudge below it, through every branch and every turn, past thousands of neighboring cells doing the same thing. Google Research built the machine-learning systems that draft those tracings, working with HHMI Janelia, the University of Cambridge, and the MRC Laboratory of Molecular Biology, whose scientists proofread and corrected the result. The imaging and tracing methods behind it were developed over decades. This particular resource went public on the eighth of June this year, and the paper followed on the third of September.

What an engineer downloads at the end of all that is a graph. Neurons are the nodes, connections are the edges, and each edge carries the number of synaptic contacts the two cells make with each other. The graph arrives with structure already in it: dedicated routes for vision, for smell, for driving legs and wings, for holding onto something that happened a moment ago. Nobody drew that layout on a whiteboard. It is there because it kept flies alive.

To make that graph respond, we give its neurons equations that describe their behavior: how much voltage builds in a modeled neuron as inputs arrive, how much charge crosses a synapse, when the cell fires, and how quickly it settles afterward. Those equations come out of the experimental literature — fly neurons have been recorded from for decades, and published spiking models of fly circuits are already there to work from. So the wiring comes out of the animal, the timing comes out of a simulator, and running the thing means running both.

Which brings us to the awkward part. A fruit fly's nervous system has no input for baseball. It has inputs for light, for odor molecules, for the taste of something sweet and the taste of something bitter, for the position of its own legs. So the first engineering problem in bet three sixty fly is the adapter. We use the taste pathways. The pregame numbers for a team — recent form, the starting pitcher, the ordinary inputs any baseball model takes — become patterns of stimulation on the food-attraction and food-avoidance pathways, an engineered version of putting something sweet or something bitter in front of the animal. Both teams are encoded by the same rules and presented separately, so one team tasting good never requires the other to taste bad, using only information available before the game.

Then you run it and watch. Activity spreads from the taste inputs through whatever the wiring allows, settles, and leaves a pattern across a set of neurons we chose in advance. That pattern is what we read out, and a small external model fit on past seasons turns the two patterns into a prediction. If you work with embeddings, the shape will be familiar: something goes in, a representation comes out of the middle, a head reads the representation. The middle here is a circuit with an anatomy, unfolding over simulated milliseconds.

Across the two thousand four hundred and twenty-three games of the 2023 Major League Baseball season, the frozen pipeline called about fifty-six percent of them correctly, level with a conventional model given the same pregame information. That gives us a season of recorded responses to investigate. Every prediction now has a path through named cells, and those cells can be addressed one at a time. You can pick a pathway, quiet it, present the same matchup again, and see what the readout does — then do it a hundred more times with a different route switched off in each run.

A living fly also carries the previous encounter into the next one. A fly that finds food while a particular smell is in the air will treat that smell differently the next time it drifts past. The structure behind that is called the mushroom body, where sensory signals meet a teaching signal carried by dopamine. In a separate development model we built that in: pair a cue with the teaching signal, wait, then present the cue on its own, and the circuit answers it differently than it did before. The change stays specific to the cue that was trained, and it reverses when the teaching signal reverses. Those learned connections give us another part of the model to investigate in the sports experiments.

Once you have an adapter into the circuit, you can give it a different environment. A developer connected the same released wiring to Doom, the first-person shooter, in a project called DOOMFLY. Game frames go in as input, the code path runs through the retained connectome graph, and a fixed set of outputs is mapped onto moving and shooting. That code path is also how you tell a project like DOOMFLY apart from the flies crawling around people's feeds, because some of those are animations — a fly wanders a browser window with a dopamine meter ticking beside it, the behavior written in JavaScript, no neural model making any of the decisions. The useful question is what controls the next movement. With DOOMFLY you can open the repository and follow one frame all the way to a keypress, which means the same interventions are available there.

In Doom, each action changes the next game frame. Turning alters the view; moving brings something closer. The circuit's outputs become part of its next input. Giving that loop the sensory detail and physical consequences of an animal's world is a larger undertaking, and researchers have been developing the pieces. FlyVis takes the measured connectivity of the fly's visual system, optimizes a model of it on visual tasks, and compares its modeled cells against recordings from real ones. FlyBody is the other end: a fly's body in a physics engine, with reinforcement-learning controllers that make it walk and fly. Those resources let researchers study what a future connectome-derived controller with eyes and legs would have to handle.

The reason all of this is arriving at once is that two very different timescales met. The imaging, the tracing, the proofreading — that is decades of method and years of work on this one specimen, released in June. The experiments built on top of it are weeks old. Reading into an unfamiliar literature, writing an adapter, getting somebody else's research code to run used to be the slow parts, and coding agents built on large language models have made them cheap enough that a small team can cross from neuroscience to a running pipeline in days.

And the maps keep improving. A larval zebrafish is transparent, which means you can point a microscope at a living one and watch neurons light up across most of its brain while the animal behaves. Google Research's neural mapping program is reconstructing the wiring of a zebrafish brain in a project called Fish2, with the aim of pairing that wiring with activity recorded from the same specimen; the companion ZAPBench dataset already carries activity from more than seventy thousand neurons. For anyone writing the dynamics, that pairing changes the work. You can run your equations on the measured wiring, hold the result against what those same neurons actually did, and tighten the model where the two disagree.

This work is extending into mammalian circuits too. The MICrONS project reconstructed a block of mouse visual cortex holding something like half a billion synaptic contacts, with activity recordings from the same tissue, and larger mapping programs are underway. Every species brings its own biology, and what carries across is the practice: writing an interface into a sensory pathway, and designing an intervention that isolates one route through a circuit.

We started with the question of what you could build around an animal's neural architecture. Now we can take a circuit shaped by finding food, present it with a baseball matchup, and examine how its old pathways respond to the new problem. As the maps grow to include more of an animal's brain and its recorded activity, we will have much more of that animal's solution available to work with. The fly is where we can start building that experience today.
